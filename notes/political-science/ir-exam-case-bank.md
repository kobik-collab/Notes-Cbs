# IR Exam Case Bank

> **How to use.** For any question, pick ONE theory as your main answer and ONE as your rebutted counter. Each row = that theory's angle, the **named concepts** to use (all from the course/notes), two empirical examples, and the weakness to expect in the counterargument. ⚠️ = forced fit (that theory struggles here; good to know for picking a *counter*, risky as a *main*).
>
> **5 theories:** Realism (structural) · Liberal institutionalism · Liberal internationalism · Constructivism · Critical theory.
> Examples are *named only* — flesh out the identity/mechanism story yourself in the essay.
>
> **The named concepts per theory (use only these):**
> - **Realism:** anarchy · self-help · security dilemma · balance of power · relative vs absolute gains · polarity · power transition · deterrence · bandwagoning.
> - **Lib. institutionalism:** absolute gains · institutions · international regime (Krasner) · the 4 mechanisms (*shadow of the future · information/monitoring · reciprocity/enforcement · interdependence*) · complex interdependence (sensitivity/vulnerability) · collective security.
> - **Lib. internationalism:** Kantian triangle (democracy + interdependence + IOs) · democratic peace · domestic preferences / opening the black box.
> - **Constructivism:** identity · norms · logic of appropriateness (vs logic of consequences) · social construction · intersubjective meaning (social vs material facts) · "anarchy is what states make of it" · socialisation.
> - **Critical theory:** hegemony (Gramsci) · class / Marxism · world-systems (core–periphery, Wallerstein) · ideology / legitimation · postcolonialism.

## ★ The core 20 — memorise THESE, reuse everywhere

Learn these 20 instead of 75. Each has the **facts you'd actually cite** + how to **frame the same event through each theory** it serves. *Fits Q* = question numbers it works for.

**1. US–USSR Cold War (1947–91).** *Fits Q6, 10, 11, 12.* Bipolar nuclear rivalry — arms race and proxy wars, then détente and arms-control treaties (SALT, INF).
• *Realism:* security dilemma + balance of power + deterrence (MAD) → intense rivalry but no direct war. • *Lib. inst.:* SALT/INF = institutions letting rivals cooperate on the one issue that matters. • *Constr.:* the two *constructed* each other as existential enemies — enmity, not just material capability.

**2. Cuban Missile Crisis (1962).** *Fits Q10, 12.* USSR put missiles in Cuba; 13-day standoff; resolved by USSR withdrawing them and US secretly pulling missiles from Turkey + pledging not to invade Cuba.
• *Realism:* brinkmanship under the shadow of nuclear war; relative power. • *Two-level games:* each leader had to sell the deal to hardliners at home — the secret Turkey swap shows domestic ratification constraints.

**3. NATO (1949–).** *Fits Q4, 7, 8.* Collective-defence alliance formed to balance the USSR — and it *survived* the USSR's collapse, enlarging eastward.
• *Realism:* balancing a threat (but its post-1991 survival is realism's embarrassment). • *Lib. inst.:* an enduring institution lowering cooperation costs. • *Constr.:* a transatlantic "security community" bound by shared democratic identity, not just threat.

**4. The EU (1951–).** *Fits Q4, 7, 10, 12.* Deep economic + political integration that made war between France and Germany "unthinkable."
• *Lib. inst.:* complex interdependence + institutions make war too costly. • *Lib. intl.:* a club of democracies (democratic peace). • *Constr.:* built a partial European identity / security community.

**5. NPT (1968).** *Fits Q2, 5, 8, 11.* Non-proliferation regime: non-nuclear states pledge not to build; the five nuclear "haves" are recognised as exceptions and pledge (vaguely) to disarm.
• *Lib. inst.:* a regime raising the cost of proliferating → most states don't. • *Critical:* a two-tier order — the powerful keep bombs, the rest give them up.

**6. Kazakhstan / Ukraine denuclearisation (Budapest Memorandum, 1994).** *Fits Q2, 11.* Both inherited huge Soviet arsenals and gave them up for security assurances + NPT membership.
• *Lib. inst.:* institutions (NPT + assurances) made giving up the capability safe. • *Realism:* they couldn't afford/secure the arsenal and took a great-power guarantee — which 2022 showed was hollow.

**7. Brazil–Argentina (ABACC, 1991).** *Fits Q2, 15.* Two rivals, both with secret nuclear programmes, democratised and built a *joint mutual-inspection agency* instead of bombs.
• *Lib. inst.:* reciprocity + verification let rivals disarm without either being exposed. • *Lib. intl.:* democratisation changed the domestic preference. • Kills critical theory's "strong coerce weak" line — these were *equals*.

**8. US–China today.** *Fits Q1, 5, 15.* A rising power challenging the incumbent: deep trade ties *and* rivalry over Taiwan, tech, the South China Sea.
• *Realism:* power transition + relative gains. • *Lib. inst.:* interdependence/WTO restrain conflict. • *Constr.:* whether they're "rivals" depends on threat framing.

**9. Russia–Ukraine / Crimea (2014, 2022).** *Fits Q1, 3, 14.* Russia — a UN founding power that helped make sovereignty sacrosanct — annexed Crimea and invaded Ukraine; hit by sweeping sanctions.
• *Realism:* balancing NATO enlargement; sphere of influence. • *Constr.:* "Russian world" identity — Ukraine as *part of* Russia's self-conception. • *Critical:* a great power rewriting rules it authored. • *Q14:* sanctions largely failed (survival + Chinese help).

**10. US–Iraq 2003.** *Fits Q3, 5, 9.* The US invaded without UN Security Council authorisation — ignoring the Charter it helped write.
• *Realism:* it had the power to. • *Constr.:* American *exceptionalism* — identity as the indispensable nation above the rules. • *Critical:* "humanitarian/WMD" cover for hegemonic/resource interests.

**11. WWI / 1914.** *Fits Q6, 10, 11, 12.* A rising Germany, rigid alliances, and an arms race ended in catastrophic war — *despite* dense pre-war trade.
• *Realism:* power transition + security dilemma. • *Liberalism's own weakness:* interdependence did **not** prevent war (use this to rebut liberalism). • *Critical:* imperial/capitalist rivalry.

**12. WTO / global trade regime.** *Fits Q5, 8, 15.* The institution that built rules-based trade — which the US, its chief architect, later undercut (tariffs, paralysing the appellate body).
• *Lib. inst.:* reputation + reciprocity → mostly obeyed. • *Realism:* the powerful follow rules only while convenient.

**13. UN Security Council veto.** *Fits Q3, 5, 8.* The five victors of WWII gave *themselves* a permanent veto.
• *Critical:* an institution literally built to serve the great powers ("who wrote the rules?"). • *Realism:* institutions mirror the distribution of power.

**14. Democratic peace (W. Europe post-1945).** *Fits Q4, 10, 12.* No two mature democracies have fought a full-scale war; former enemies (France–Germany) became close partners.
• *Lib. intl.:* Kantian triangle — accountability, interdependence, IOs. • *Realist counter:* it was shared interests + the US alliance, not democracy.

**15. Rwanda 1994 vs Somalia 1992 / Libya 2011.** *Fits Q9.* The world did *not* intervene in the Rwandan genocide but *did* in Somalia and Libya.
• *Constr.:* widening (and selective) norms of who "deserves" protection. • *Realism:* intervention tracks strategic interest — Rwanda had none.

**16. Iran nuclear deal / JCPOA (2015).** *Fits Q5, 14.* Iran capped its programme for sanctions relief; the US later withdrew (2018).
• *Lib. inst.:* an institution/regime trading verification for relief. • *Two-level games:* the US ratification fight (and withdrawal) shows Level-II domestic politics undoing a Level-I deal.

**17. North Korea.** *Fits Q5, 11, 14.* Built nuclear weapons for regime survival, *left* the NPT (2003), and has absorbed years of sanctions.
• *Realism:* self-help + deterrence for survival under anarchy. • *Q14:* sanctions failed — survival trumps economic pain.

**18. South Africa.** *Fits Q2.* The only state to *build* nuclear weapons and then voluntarily *dismantle* them (c. 1989–91), joining the NPT as apartheid ended.
• *Lib. intl.:* a new domestic regime changed the preference. • *Constr.:* a new post-apartheid identity made the bomb inappropriate.

**19. China & the South China Sea (2016 ruling).** *Fits Q3, 5, 15.* China ratified the Law of the Sea, then ignored the 2016 tribunal ruling against its claims.
• *Realism:* a rising power defies law when strong enough. • *Critical:* the order's rules bind the weak, not the powerful.

**20. Trump "America First" (2017–).** *Fits Q3, 13.* Withdrawals (Paris, JCPOA, WHO) and tariffs driven by domestic politics.
• *Lib. intl. / FPA / two-level games:* domestic coalitions and leaders — not the system — drove the rule-breaking. • Good for "why states act against the systemic 'national interest'."

**Under pressure:** pick the question → grab 2 examples whose *Fits Q* includes it → tell each one's facts, then frame through your theory. Two of these, done well, answer almost any question.

## 1. Why do rival states cooperate in one area while staying hostile in another?

| Theory | Explanation (1-line) | Concepts (named) | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Cooperate where it doesn't shift the relative balance; hostile where it does | relative vs absolute gains, balance of power, self-help | Russia–EU gas (vs NATO); India–China trade (vs border) | Struggles to explain *durable, institutionalised* cooperation |
| Lib. institutionalism | Institutions make cooperation safe in one domain | institutions, international regime, reciprocity | US–USSR arms control; US–China climate talks amid trade war | Can't explain persisting hostility |
| Lib. internationalism ⚠️ | Domestic preferences favour cooperation on some issues, not others | domestic preferences, the black box | US–China (business lobby vs security hawks) | Thin on *why rivalry persists*; forced |
| Constructivism | Different shared meanings/identities built per issue-area | identity, intersubjective meaning, "anarchy is what states make of it" | US–China (partner vs rival framing) | Weak at predicting *which* area turns cooperative |
| Critical theory | Cooperation serves dominant economic interests; hostility masks it | hegemony, class, world-systems | Russia–EU energy capital; Gulf states–West | Hard to test; over-reads everything as capital |

## 2. Why do some states abandon a military capability they've already developed?

| Theory | Explanation | Concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Abandon when a guarantee/alliance covers the need, or coerced | deterrence, balance of power, self-help | Kazakhstan/Ukraine (assurances); Germany/Japan post-45 | Distrusts institutions — awkward with voluntary treaty disarmament |
| Lib. institutionalism | Institutions make verified mutual disarmament safe | reciprocity/enforcement, information/monitoring, international regime | Brazil–Argentina (ABACC); Kazakhstan (NPT) | Can't explain defection (North Korea leaving NPT) |
| Lib. internationalism | A new domestic coalition (democratisation) drops the programme | domestic preferences, the black box, democratic peace | Brazil & Argentina (post-dictatorship); South Africa | Needs a domestic shift — silent when there isn't one |
| Constructivism | A norm (non-proliferation) reshapes identity | norms, socialisation, logic of appropriateness | nuclear taboo; South Africa's post-apartheid identity | Weak where disarmament is clearly coerced |
| Critical theory | The strong pressure the weak to disarm while keeping their own | hegemony, world-systems (core–periphery) | NPT haves/have-nots; Libya/Iraq WMD disarmament | Can't explain equal-power mutual disarmament (Brazil–Argentina) |

## 3. Why are great powers more likely than small states to break rules they helped create?

| Theory | Explanation | Concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Relative power lets them ignore rules; small states can't | relative gains, self-help, polarity | US–Iraq 2003; Russia–Crimea/Ukraine | Can't explain why great powers *ever* obey |
| Lib. institutionalism | Great powers depend on institutions less, so the costs bite less | complex interdependence (vulnerability), reciprocity | US tariffs/WTO; US & ICC | Under-predicts how often great powers *do* comply |
| Lib. internationalism ⚠️ | Domestic politics (nationalism, executives) push rule-breaking | domestic preferences, the black box | Trump "America First"; Brexit | Doesn't track the *great/small* split cleanly; forced |
| Constructivism | Great powers' identity = exceptional/above the rules they authored | identity, logic of appropriateness | US exceptionalism (Iraq, ICC); Russia's great-power self-image | Must show identity, not just the act (reads as realism) |
| Critical theory | The order is built to serve the powerful; "rules" bind only the weak | hegemony, ideology/legitimation | UNSC veto; US & ICC exemption | Hard to falsify |

## 4. Why do democracies rarely fight each other?

| Theory | Explanation | Concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism ⚠️ | Not democracy — shared interests, alliances, Cold War structure | balance of power, polarity | NATO democracies vs USSR; US–UK alliance | Denies a robust empirical finding; defensive |
| Lib. institutionalism | Institutions + interdependence make war too costly | complex interdependence, institutions, absolute gains | EU members; Bretton Woods order | Doesn't explain the *democracy-specific* pattern |
| Lib. internationalism | Accountability, public opinion restrain war between republics | Kantian triangle, democratic peace | no two mature democracies fought (20th c.); EU | "Democracy" is contested; edge-case wars exist |
| Constructivism | Democracies share an in-group identity — war feels inappropriate | identity, norms, logic of appropriateness | transatlantic community; EU "security community" | Hard to measure "identity"; risks circularity |
| Critical theory ⚠️ | "Democratic peace" masks shared capitalist-class interest | class, hegemony, ideology | Western bloc cohesion; global capital order | Strains against the clean empirical record |

## 5. Why do states comply with international law no one can enforce?

| Theory | Explanation | Concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Only when it serves interests / the powerful want it | self-help, balance of power | UNCLOS when convenient; great-power selectivity | Can't explain costly compliance |
| Lib. institutionalism | Reputation + reciprocity + shadow of the future make it pay | reciprocity/enforcement, shadow of the future, international regime | WTO rulings mostly obeyed; trade law | Weak vs great-power defection |
| Lib. internationalism | Domestic courts/groups internalise and demand compliance | domestic preferences, the black box | ECHR in domestic courts; EU law | Varies hugely by regime type |
| Constructivism | Law is internalised as "appropriate" | norms, logic of appropriateness, socialisation | decolonisation norm; torture taboo | Can't explain brazen violations |
| Critical theory | Law legitimises the powerful; compliance = consent to hegemony | hegemony, ideology/legitimation | investment/trade law favouring the core | Over-reads; hard to test |

## 6. Why do arms races happen?

| Theory | Explanation | Concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Defensive build-ups look offensive under anarchy | security dilemma, self-help, relative gains | US–USSR Cold War; pre-1914 naval race | Can't explain restraint/de-escalation |
| Lib. institutionalism | Failure of institutions to provide information/reassurance | information/monitoring, international regime | arms-control treaties that *stopped* races (INF) | Explains the *fix* better than the *race* |
| Lib. internationalism | Domestic lobbies (military-industrial) drive it | domestic preferences, the black box | US defence lobby; Cold War procurement | Needs a specific domestic driver |
| Constructivism ⚠️ | Rival identities make the other's arms seem threatening | identity, intersubjective meaning (social vs material) | India–Pakistan rivalry; US–USSR enmity | Thin on *why build-ups spiral* |
| Critical theory | Arms racing serves capital/the militarist order | class, hegemony | arms-industry profits; Cold War economy | Hard to falsify |

## 7. Why do states form alliances?

| Theory | Explanation | Concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Balance against a greater threat (or bandwagon with it) | balance of power, bandwagoning, polarity | NATO vs USSR; anti-Napoleon coalitions | Struggles with alliance *persistence* after threat ends (NATO post-91) |
| Lib. institutionalism | Alliances as institutions lowering cooperation costs | institutions, reciprocity, collective security | NATO's enduring structure; EU defence | Under-weights raw threat |
| Lib. internationalism | Shared regime type / domestic affinity | democratic peace, domestic preferences | NATO democracies; US–UK | Doesn't explain alliances *with* autocracies |
| Constructivism | Shared identity/community, not just threat | identity, "security community" | transatlantic community; ASEAN | Weak when alliances are purely instrumental |
| Critical theory | Alliances entrench the hegemon's order | hegemony, world-systems | US alliance system; client states | Over-reads; hard to test |

## 8. Why do international institutions persist even when they can't compel states?

| Theory | Explanation | Concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | They persist only while they serve the powerful | self-help, balance of power | UNSC veto; IMF weighted votes | Can't explain institutions outliving the founder's interest |
| Lib. institutionalism | They lower transaction costs, solve collective-action problems | institutions, international regime, shadow of the future | WTO; WHO; UN system | Over-optimistic vs great-power sidelining |
| Lib. internationalism | Domestic groups benefit and lobby to keep them | domestic preferences, the black box | EU constituencies; trade lobbies | Varies by state |
| Constructivism | They become taken-for-granted, part of "normal" order | norms, socialisation, intersubjective meaning | the UN's legitimacy; sovereignty norm | Can't explain sudden collapses |
| Critical theory | They reproduce the hegemonic order and look "natural" | hegemony (Gramsci), ideology/legitimation | IMF/World Bank conditionality; Bretton Woods | Hard to falsify |

## 10. Why do some rivalries end in war while others stay peaceful?

| Theory | Explanation | Concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Power shifts / security dilemma tip rivalry into war | power transition, security dilemma | WWI (rising Germany); US–USSR *avoided* war (deterrence) | Indeterminate — predicts both |
| Lib. institutionalism | Institutions + interdependence raise war's cost | complex interdependence, institutions | EU ending Franco-German war; 1914 (failed) | Interdependence didn't stop 1914 |
| Lib. internationalism | Regime type — democracies avoid war with each other | democratic peace, Kantian triangle | Western Europe post-45 | Edge cases |

## 13. Why do states sometimes act against their material interests?

| Theory | Explanation | Concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism ⚠️ | They don't, really — apparent losses serve power/security | relative gains, self-help | costly signalling; alliance commitments | Explains the behaviour away rather than *for* it |
| Lib. institutionalism | Short-term cost for long-term reciprocal gain | shadow of the future, reciprocity, absolute gains | sunk reputational costs; treaty compliance | Stretches "interest" to fit |
| Lib. internationalism | A domestic coalition's interest ≠ the "national" one | domestic preferences, the black box | tariffs hurting consumers; farm subsidies | Needs the coalition identified |
| Constructivism | Identity/norms override material calculation | identity, logic of appropriateness, norms | Germany's post-45 restraint; costly norm compliance | Hard to prove norms *caused* it |
| Critical theory | "National interest" masks a *class* interest being served | class, ideology/legitimation | austerity serving capital | Hard to falsify |

## 14. Why do economic sanctions often fail?

| Theory | Explanation | Concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Targets prioritise survival; rivals help them | self-help, balance of power | Russia post-2014/2022; North Korea | Under-weights when sanctions *do* work |
| Lib. institutionalism | Work only with multilateral coordination/monitoring | collective security, information/monitoring | UN vs unilateral sanctions; leaky regimes | Hard to sustain coordination |
| Lib. internationalism | The target's regime insulates elites from pain | domestic preferences, the black box | authoritarian resilience (Iran, NK) | Varies by regime |
| Constructivism | Sanctions can harden a besieged national identity | identity, norms | Iran; Russia's "fortress" narrative | Hard to measure |
| Critical theory | Sanctions reinforce core dominance; hurt the periphery's poor | world-systems (core–periphery), hegemony | sanctions' humanitarian toll | Doesn't explain *failure* vs success |

## 15. Why do power transitions (rising powers) cause conflict?

| Theory | Explanation | Concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | A riser unsettles the balance → security dilemma, war risk | power transition, balance of power, security dilemma | US–China today; rising Germany pre-1914 | Many transitions *don't* end in war |
| Lib. institutionalism | Institutions can socialise a riser peacefully | institutions, complex interdependence | China into WTO; post-45 order | Riser may defect once strong |
| Lib. internationalism | Depends on the riser's regime type | democratic peace, domestic preferences | democratic vs autocratic risers | Hard to generalise |
| Constructivism | Conflict if the riser is constructed as a threatening "other" | identity, intersubjective meaning | US–China threat framing; UK→US peaceful transition | Why peaceful sometimes? identity under-determines |
| Critical theory | Transition = struggle over who controls the capitalist order | hegemony, world-systems | British→American hegemony; US–China | Coarse; hard to test |

### Quick picking guide
- **Security / war / weapons** (6, 10, 11, 12, 15) → **realism** strongest main, **constructivism** or **liberalism** as counter.
- **Cooperation / law / institutions** (1, 2, 5, 8) → **liberalism** main, **realism** or **critical** counter.
- **Identity / norms / "why against interest"** (4, 13) → **constructivism** main, **realism** counter.
- **Who-benefits / hierarchy** (3, 9) → **critical theory** makes a sharp counter; realism/constructivism as main.
- ⚠️ rows = the theory to attack in your rebuttal, not to defend as your main.
