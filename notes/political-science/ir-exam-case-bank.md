# IR Exam Case Bank

> **How to use.** For any question, pick ONE theory as your main answer and ONE as your rebutted counter. Each row = that theory's angle, the concepts to name, two empirical examples, and the weakness to expect in the counterargument. ⚠️ = forced fit (that theory struggles here; good to know for picking a *counter*, risky as a *main*).
>
> **5 theories:** Realism (structural) · Liberal institutionalism · Liberal internationalism · Constructivism · Critical theory.
> Examples are *named only* — flesh out the identity/mechanism story yourself in the essay.

## 1. Why do rival states cooperate in one area while staying hostile in another?

| Theory | Explanation (1-line) | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Cooperate where it doesn't shift the relative balance; hostile where it does | relative vs absolute gains, balance of power, self-help | Russia–EU gas (vs NATO); India–China trade (vs border) | Struggles to explain *durable, institutionalised* cooperation |
| Lib. institutionalism | Issue-specific institutions make cooperation safe in one domain | institutions, reciprocity, issue-areas | US–USSR arms control (Cold War); US–China climate talks amid trade war | Can't explain persisting hostility |
| Lib. internationalism ⚠️ | Domestic coalitions favour cooperation on some issues, not others | preferences, domestic interests | US–China (business lobby vs security hawks) | Thin on *why rivalry persists*; forced |
| Constructivism | Different shared meanings/identities built per issue-area | identity, norms, intersubjective meaning | US–China (economic partner vs strategic rival framing) | Weak at predicting *which* area turns cooperative |
| Critical theory | Cooperation serves dominant economic interests; hostility masks it | hegemony, class/capital, core–periphery | Russia–EU energy capital flows; Gulf states–West | Hard to test; over-reads everything as capital |

## 2. Why do some states abandon a military capability they've already developed?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Abandon when a guarantee/alliance covers the need, or coerced | security guarantee, extended deterrence, relative power | Kazakhstan/Ukraine (assurances); Germany/Japan post-45 | Distrusts institutions — awkward with voluntary treaty disarmament |
| Lib. institutionalism | Institutions make verified mutual disarmament safe | reciprocity, monitoring, NPT/regimes | Brazil–Argentina (ABACC); Kazakhstan (NPT) | Can't explain defection (North Korea leaving NPT) |
| Lib. internationalism | New domestic coalition (democratisation) drops the programme | regime change, preferences | Brazil & Argentina (post-dictatorship); South Africa | Needs a domestic shift — silent when there isn't one |
| Constructivism | A norm (non-proliferation taboo) reshapes identity | norms, taboo, socialisation | nuclear taboo; South Africa's post-apartheid identity | Weak where disarmament is clearly coerced |
| Critical theory | The strong pressure the weak to disarm while keeping their own | hierarchy, two-tier order, hegemony | NPT haves/have-nots; Libya/Iraq WMD disarmament | Can't explain equal-power mutual disarmament (Brazil–Argentina) |

## 3. Why are great powers more likely than small states to break rules they helped create?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | They can — relative power lets them ignore rules; small states can't | relative power, self-help, hegemony | US–Iraq 2003; Russia–Crimea/Ukraine | Can't explain why great powers *ever* obey |
| Lib. institutionalism | Great powers depend on institutions less, so costs of breaking bite less | dependence, reputation, reciprocity | US tariffs/WTO; US & ICC | Under-predicts how often great powers *do* comply |
| Lib. internationalism ⚠️ | Domestic politics (nationalism, executives) push rule-breaking | domestic coalitions, executive power | Trump "America First"; Brexit | Doesn't track the *great/small* split cleanly; forced |
| Constructivism | Great powers' identity = exceptional/above the rules they authored | identity, exceptionalism, logic of appropriateness | US exceptionalism (Iraq, ICC); Russia's "great power" self-image | Must show identity, not just the act (reads as realism) |
| Critical theory | The order is built to serve the powerful; "rules" bind only the weak | hegemony, who-writes-the-rules | UNSC veto; US & ICC exemption | Hard to falsify |

## 4. Why do democracies rarely fight each other?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism ⚠️ | Not democracy — shared interests, alliances, Cold War structure | balance of power, alliances, polarity | NATO democracies vs USSR; US–UK alliance | Denies a robust empirical finding; defensive |
| Lib. institutionalism | Dense institutions + interdependence make war too costly | interdependence, IOs, absolute gains | EU members; Bretton Woods order | Doesn't explain the *democracy-specific* pattern |
| Lib. internationalism | Accountability, public opinion, transparency restrain war | Kantian triangle, democratic peace, accountability | no two mature democracies fought (20th c.); EU | "Democracy" is contested; edge-case wars exist |
| Constructivism | Democracies share an in-group identity — war feels inappropriate | identity, shared norms, "we-feeling" | transatlantic community; EU "security community" | Hard to measure "identity"; risks circularity |
| Critical theory ⚠️ | "Democratic peace" masks shared capitalist-class interest | capital, hegemony, ideology | Western bloc cohesion; global capital order | Strains against the clean empirical record |

## 5. Why do states comply with international law no one can enforce?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Only when it serves interests / the powerful want it | self-help, power, hegemony | UNCLOS when convenient; great-power selectivity | Can't explain costly compliance |
| Lib. institutionalism | Reputation + reciprocity + shadow of the future make it pay | reciprocity, reputation, regimes | WTO rulings mostly obeyed; trade law | Weak vs great-power defection |
| Lib. internationalism | Domestic courts/groups internalise and demand compliance | domestic institutions, preferences | ECHR in domestic courts; EU law | Varies hugely by regime type |
| Constructivism | Law is internalised as "appropriate"; identity as law-abiding | norms, logic of appropriateness, legitimacy | decolonisation norm; torture taboo | Can't explain brazen violations |
| Critical theory | Law legitimises the powerful; compliance = consent to hegemony | hegemony, legitimation, ideology | investment/trade law favouring the core | Over-reads; hard to test |

## 6. Why do arms races happen?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Security dilemma — defensive build-ups look offensive under anarchy | security dilemma, self-help, relative gains | US–USSR Cold War; pre-1914 naval race | Can't explain restraint/de-escalation |
| Lib. institutionalism | Failure of institutions to provide information/reassurance | information, monitoring, regimes | arms-control treaties that *stopped* races (INF) | Explains the *fix* better than the *race* |
| Lib. internationalism | Domestic lobbies (military-industrial complex) drive it | interest groups, domestic preferences | US defence lobby; Cold War procurement | Needs a specific domestic driver |
| Constructivism ⚠️ | Rival identities make the other's arms seem threatening | identity, enmity, social facts | India–Pakistan rivalry; US–USSR enmity | Thin on *why build-ups spiral* |
| Critical theory | Arms racing serves capital/the military-industrial order | capital, militarism, class | arms industry profits; Cold War economy | Hard to falsify |

## 7. Why do states form alliances?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Balance against a greater threat (or bandwagon with it) | balance of power, threat, bandwagoning | NATO vs USSR; anti-Napoleon coalitions | Struggles with alliance *persistence* after threat ends (NATO post-91) |
| Lib. institutionalism | Alliances as institutions lowering cooperation costs | institutions, reciprocity, credible commitment | NATO's enduring structure; EU defence | Under-weights raw threat |
| Lib. internationalism | Shared regime type/domestic affinity | democratic peace, preferences | NATO democracies; US–UK | Doesn't explain alliances *with* autocracies |
| Constructivism | Shared identity/community, not just threat | identity, security community | transatlantic community; ASEAN | Weak when alliances are purely instrumental |
| Critical theory | Alliances entrench the hegemon's order | hegemony, core, dependency | US alliance system; client states | Over-reads; hard to test |

## 8. Why do international institutions persist even when they can't compel states?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | They persist only while they serve the powerful | power, hegemony, instrument | UNSC veto; IMF weighted votes | Can't explain institutions outliving their founder's interest |
| Lib. institutionalism | They lower transaction costs, solve collective-action problems | regimes, reciprocity, info, shadow of future | WTO; WHO; UN system | Over-optimistic vs great-power sidelining |
| Lib. internationalism | Domestic groups benefit and lobby to keep them | domestic preferences, embeddedness | EU constituencies; trade lobbies | Varies by state |
| Constructivism | They become taken-for-granted, part of "normal" order | norms, legitimacy, institutionalisation | the UN's legitimacy; sovereignty norm | Can't explain sudden collapses |
| Critical theory | They reproduce the hegemonic order and look "natural" | hegemony, legitimation, Gramsci | IMF/World Bank conditionality; Bretton Woods | Hard to falsify |

## 9. Why do states (sometimes) intervene in humanitarian crises?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Only when strategic interests coincide; otherwise they don't | national interest, power | Libya 2011 (interest-aligned); Rwanda 1994 (non-intervention) | Can't explain genuinely costly intervention |
| Lib. institutionalism | Collective action via IOs/authorisation | collective security, IOs, R2P | UN-authorised missions; R2P | Weak when IOs are paralysed |
| Lib. internationalism | Public opinion/domestic pressure drives it | public opinion, accountability | "CNN effect"; democratic publics | Inconsistent across cases |
| Constructivism | Widening norms of who "deserves" protection | norms, identity, logic of appropriateness | Somalia 1992; R2P norm emergence | Can't explain non-intervention (Rwanda) |
| Critical theory | "Humanitarian" cover for hegemonic/economic interests | hegemony, imperialism critique | Iraq/Libya resource angle | Cynical; hard to test |

## 10. Why do some rivalries end in war while others stay peaceful?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Power shifts / security dilemma tip rivalry into war | power transition, security dilemma | WWI (rising Germany); US–USSR *avoided* war (MAD) | Indeterminate — predicts both |
| Lib. institutionalism | Institutions + interdependence raise war's cost | interdependence, regimes | EU ending Franco-German war; 1914 (failed) | Interdependence didn't stop 1914 |
| Lib. internationalism | Regime type — democracies avoid war with each other | democratic peace, accountability | Western Europe post-45; India–Pakistan (not democracies-pair) | Edge cases |
| Constructivism | Whether the rival is constructed as enemy or competitor | enmity, identity, "anarchy is what states make of it" | US–Canada (amity) vs US–USSR (enmity) | Hard to predict ex ante |
| Critical theory ⚠️ | War/peace reflects material/class structures | capital, imperialism | WWI imperial rivalry | Coarse; hard to test |

## 11. Why do states pursue nuclear weapons?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Security under anarchy; deterrence vs a threat | self-help, deterrence, security dilemma | North Korea; Pakistan (vs India) | Can't explain restraint by capable states (Japan) |
| Lib. institutionalism | Regimes (NPT) raise costs → most *don't* | NPT, regimes, reciprocity | NPT near-universal membership | Explains non-pursuit better than pursuit |
| Lib. internationalism | Domestic politics/prestige coalitions | domestic interests, prestige | India's domestic drivers | Needs case-specific coalition |
| Constructivism | Prestige/identity (a "great power has the bomb") or taboo restrains | identity, prestige, nuclear taboo | France's force de frappe; non-nuclear norm | Competing identity claims cut both ways |
| Critical theory ⚠️ | The order lets the core keep bombs, denies the periphery | hierarchy, two-tier NPT | NPT haves vs have-nots | Doesn't explain *why* a given state builds |

## 12. Why has great-power war declined since 1945?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Nuclear deterrence (MAD) + bipolar stability | deterrence, polarity, balance of power | US–USSR Cold War peace; MAD | Can't explain peace among non-nuclear democracies |
| Lib. institutionalism | Dense institutions + interdependence raise costs | interdependence, IOs, regimes | EU; Bretton Woods/WTO order | Didn't stop proxy wars |
| Lib. internationalism | Spread of democracy → democratic peace | democratic peace, Kantian triangle | post-45 Western Europe | Doesn't cover autocracies' restraint |
| Constructivism | A norm against great-power war took hold | norms, taboo, learning | WWI/WWII "never again"; war delegitimised | Hard to isolate the norm's effect |
| Critical theory | US hegemony imposed a stable (profitable) order | hegemony, Pax Americana | Bretton Woods; dollar order | Over-credits structure |

## 13. Why do states sometimes act against their material interests?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism ⚠️ | They don't, really — apparent losses serve power/security | national interest, relative gains | costly signalling; alliance commitments | Explains the behaviour away rather than *for* it |
| Lib. institutionalism | Short-term cost for long-term reciprocal gain | shadow of the future, reciprocity | sunk reputational costs; treaty compliance | Stretches "interest" to fit |
| Lib. internationalism | A domestic coalition's interest ≠ the "national" one | domestic preferences, lobbies | tariffs hurting consumers; farm subsidies | Needs the coalition identified |
| Constructivism | Identity/norms override material calculation | identity, logic of appropriateness, norms | Germany's restraint; costly norm compliance | Hard to prove norms *caused* it |
| Critical theory | "National interest" masks a *class* interest being served | class, ideology, hegemony | austerity serving capital | Hard to falsify |

## 14. Why do economic sanctions often fail?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Targets prioritise survival over economic pain; rivals help them | self-help, survival, balancing | Russia post-2014/2022; North Korea | Under-weights when sanctions *do* work |
| Lib. institutionalism | Work only with multilateral coordination/monitoring | collective action, regimes, monitoring | UN vs unilateral sanctions; leaky regimes | Hard to sustain coordination |
| Lib. internationalism | Target's domestic regime insulates elites from pain | regime type, domestic structure | authoritarian resilience (Iran, NK) | Varies by regime |
| Constructivism | Sanctions can harden a besieged national identity | identity, rally-round-the-flag | Iran; Russia's "fortress" narrative | Hard to measure |
| Critical theory | Sanctions reinforce core dominance; hurt the periphery's poor | hierarchy, dependency | sanctions' humanitarian toll | Doesn't explain *failure* vs success |

## 15. Why do power transitions (rising powers) cause conflict?

| Theory | Explanation | Key concepts | 2 examples | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realism | Rising power unsettles the balance → security dilemma, war risk | power transition, balance of power, Thucydides trap | US–China today; rising Germany pre-1914 | Many transitions *don't* end in war |
| Lib. institutionalism | Institutions can socialise a riser peacefully | regimes, integration, interdependence | China into WTO; post-45 order | Riser may defect once strong |
| Lib. internationalism | Depends on the riser's regime type/domestic politics | regime type, preferences | democratic vs autocratic risers | Hard to generalise |
| Constructivism | Conflict if the riser is constructed as a threatening "other" | identity, enmity, status | US–China threat framing; UK–US peaceful transition | Why peaceful sometimes? identity under-determines |
| Critical theory | Transition = struggle over who controls the capitalist order | hegemony, world-systems | British→American hegemony; US–China | Coarse; hard to test |

---

### Quick picking guide
- **Security / war / weapons** (6, 10, 11, 12, 15) → **realism** strongest main, **constructivism** or **liberalism** as counter.
- **Cooperation / law / institutions** (1, 2, 5, 8) → **liberalism** main, **realism** or **critical** counter.
- **Identity / norms / "why against interest"** (4, 13) → **constructivism** main, **realism** counter.
- **Who-benefits / hierarchy** (3, 9) → **critical theory** makes a sharp counter; realism/constructivism as main.
- ⚠️ rows = the theory to attack in your rebuttal, not to defend as your main.
